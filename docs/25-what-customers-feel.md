# 25. What customers feel, and what we do about it

Written 11 October 2026, with the decisions in `23` and `24`. The question was
how to make TinyFloor feel better than its feature list, without limits that
make it feel like a meter running.

## The person we're selling to

Someone on a small remote team (2–10 people) who misses working next to
each other. They try TinyFloor alone first, decide in minutes whether it
feels right, and only then bring the team. That's why 22 of 28 offices have
one member: people who tried it and didn't stay, not one-person companies.
Until the team trial existed, a team of four couldn't move in at all.

So two moments matter: **the first few minutes alone**, and **the day the
team arrives**. Pricing only matters at the second.

## Principles, and what each changed

### 1. Never show a meter

People overpay for a flat price to avoid feeling a meter run (the "taxi-meter
effect": Lambrecht & Skiera, *Paying Too Much and Being Happy About It*,
2006). And when each use feels like it costs something, people use less
(Prelec & Loewenstein, *The Red and the Black*, 1998). For a presence product,
less use means less value, which means churn.

- **Done:** no meeting-hours meter anywhere: not on Meetings, not in Plan and
  billing, not on the pricing page, not in the offer. Pro says "Meetings as
  often as your team needs".
- The backstop stays, in the terms only, as fair use: past 120 hours of
  meetings a month on Pro (40 on Free), meetings go voice only until the 1st.
  That's 5½ hours of meetings every working day; no normal team gets near it,
  and nobody sees it until it happens.

### 2. Guardrails that are features

The way to afford "no meter" is to make heavy use cheaper in ways people
like:

- **Cameras rest when it's quiet.** After 10 minutes with nobody talking, your
  camera turns off, with one press to bring it back. Video-call fatigue is
  real and mostly from being watched (Bailenson, *Nonverbal Overload*, 2021);
  this is a wellbeing feature we can say out loud: *TinyFloor won't keep you
  on camera when nobody's talking.*
- **No video to a hidden tab.** Leave the office in a background tab all day
  and you receive nothing you can't see. Lighter on laptops, batteries and
  networks, and the biggest saving we have.
- **Floor calls counted, never limited**, so a heavy office can be seen and
  understood, instead of guessed at.

### 3. One limit, at the moment it feels natural

Free's one limit is people: 3. It isn't a crippled product, it's everything
for a small group, and the limit lands exactly when the product becomes
valuable: the team arriving. That moment starts a 14-day Pro trial for
everyone, no card. The whole team lives in Pro before anyone decides, so
choosing Pro is keeping something, not buying something (endowment; loss
aversion).

### 4. Small on purpose, said as a choice

Pro is for a small team, up to 15. Small offices are cheaper for us (`24`) and
better on the floor (25 people looked like chaos). It isn't hidden: hiding
a limit someone pays for brings refunds and chargebacks, and Creem reviews
stores for it. But it's never the pitch either:

- **Everywhere people decide** (pricing cards, offer, checkout): "your whole
  team". No number.
- **Where people look for it** (FAQ, the pricing page's notes, terms): "Made
  for small teams: up to 15 people. A bigger company gives each team an
  office of its own."
- **In the app**: free offices see "1 of 3". Paid offices see who's in it, not
  how many more they may have; the seat count appears only when they're two
  away from full.

"Made for small teams" is identity, not restriction: the 6-person team reads
it as *this is for us*, and the 60-person company sorts itself out.

### 5. The price in the buyer's terms

(Built in `23`.) Per person for their team, per day when there's no team yet,
next to what per-seat tools would charge; the price on the button; "Cancel
any time. Full refund within 14 days." under it. No countdowns, crossed-out
prices or invented popularity.

## What would make it feel better still (not built)

In order of how much they'd move the two moments:

1. **Your own desk.** Everyone gets a desk the day they join and arrives
   sitting at it. It makes the floor read like an office, fixes the crowd
   problem, and gives people something that's theirs (people value what they
   helped make: the "IKEA effect").
2. **Someone there in the first minute.** Someone trying it alone meets an
   empty floor. The demo office with a few friendly regulars, or a short
   guided walk-up, shows the magic moment (walking up to someone and just
   talking) before there's a team to do it with.
3. **Invite as part of making the office**, not after: "Who do you sit next
   to?" with their emails or a link, so the first session ends with the team
   on its way.
4. **Trial reminders** 3 days and 1 day before the trial ends, saying what
   changes on the day. The end of a trial is remembered more than its middle
   (peak-end rule), so end it on something clear, not a surprise.
5. **Say the wellbeing features out loud** on the site: camera off by
   default, cameras rest when it's quiet, no recordings, walk-up calls that
   never count. They're the opposite of the meeting-heavy tools people are
   tired of.
6. **Real social proof**, once there is any: one quote from a team, a
   count of teams. Never invented.
